The reference framework for educators' digital competence across the EU, and the one your institution most likely already maps to. It predates generative systems. Its categories still hold; its examples do not reach the problems in Block 2.

▤DigComp 2.2 · ICT-CFTCitizens · and the digital-professional strandwhat they add

DigComp 2.2 (2022) added examples concerning AI systems to the citizen framework. UNESCO's ICT-CFT (2018) contributed the three-level grammar — acquisition, deepening, creation — that the later frameworks reuse. Between them they cover the citizen and the digital-professional; neither addresses your lecture theatre.

▤AI GO! (2025)newNpuls · education professionals, including universitieswhy this one is different

AI Literacy in Education — a practical framework. Built on an umbrella review and refined in practitioner workshops. Its addressee is the education professional in vocational colleges, universities of applied sciences and research universities — which is to say, you.

It is the closest published thing to the framework the next card says is missing, and it is honest to say so. What it does not carry is the German legal and examination-regulation context, which is exactly the difference a local adaptation has to write down.

▤Reference Framework 2.0newNpuls · responsible use of education data and AIwhat it is for

The governance companion: written for the institution rather than the individual. Useful when the conversation moves from what you should be able to do to what your faculty has to have in place before you can do it. Take it to the committee, not to the lecture theatre.

▤Wissenschaftsrat (2026)newIntellektuelle Souveränität · the German national framethe one sentence that matters

Not a competence framework but a set of recommendations, and the reference point a German colleague is most likely to have read. Its guiding idea is intellectual sovereignty: the capacity to judge independently, critically and with reasons in an algorithmically shaped world.

The sentence to carry into a faculty meeting: the central question is not how higher education is designed with generative AI, but how good higher education is designed in a world shaped by it. That is the same move this session makes when it puts reflection last rather than first.

Drs. 3319-26, July 2026. There is a short version if the full text is more than the meeting needs.

▲The gapNone of them was written for a university lecturerwhy this matters

Learner-facing, school-teacher-facing, citizen-facing, digital-professional-facing. The higher education teaching role — with its research obligations, its examination regulations, its academic freedom and its doctoral supervision — sits between them.

That gap is the argument for local adaptation. It is also the reason to be sceptical of any document that claims to have closed it, including the one in the next card.

One qualification, added in v4. AI GO! narrows this gap considerably: it is addressed to education professionals in higher education and it is published. The residue is national — examination regulations, the German legal frame, the supervision relationship. That residue is small enough to write down, which is the whole argument for local adaptation and against writing a new framework from scratch.

Why the outputs cannot simply be trusted

Two failure classes. One is famous and easy to catch. The other survives checking, because it acts on you rather than on the text.

✦FabricationThe confident wrong citationwhy it is the easy one

A generated reference list can be checked in five minutes, and everyone now knows to check it. This failure is architectural rather than a bug to be patched: the system produces text that fits, and fitting is not the same relation as being true. It is also the failure your students have already been warned about, which is precisely why it is no longer the one to spend a session on.

▲HomogenisationIndividually fine, collectively identicalwhat it looks like

Thirty essays that are each acceptable and share a structure, a register and a choice of example. And your own module descriptions, after a term of drafting them the same way.

▲Illusion of consensusA contested field delivered without its seamswhat it looks like

A fluent synthesis of a live disagreement. The disagreement is not reported as absent. It is simply not reported.

▲Automation complacencyThe tenth output is checked less than the firstwhat it looks like

Verification effort decays with each output that turns out to be fine. The failure rate has not changed; your sampling of it has.

▲Confirmation and anchoringYou get the framing you supplied, then keep itwhat it looks like

Ask why a method is appropriate and you will receive reasons, because that is what you asked for. And the first draft sets the structure of everything after it, including the parts you write yourself.

▲SycophancynewAgreement, mistaken for confirmationwhy warning people does not fix it

These systems are measurably biased towards validating what the user has already said. Put a hypothesis to one and you will be told it is interesting; put the opposite hypothesis to it an hour later and you will be told the same.

The finding worth knowing. Chandra and colleagues (2026) model a conversation between a chatbot and an ideal Bayesian user — a hypothetical person who updates perfectly rationally and should therefore be immune. Even that user spirals into unwarranted confidence, and the effect survives two obvious fixes: removing the system’s false claims, and telling the user in advance that it is sycophantic.

For teaching, the consequence is narrow and sharp. “I checked it with the AI” is not a check. And a colleague who has been warned is not thereby protected, which is why the response has to be procedural — a second reader, a disconfirming prompt, a source — rather than an attitude.

▲The learning stallnewThe difficulty was the lessonwhat it looks like in a student

A student who never reaches the point of productive difficulty never gets the thing that difficulty produces. The work arrives finished, the confidence arrives with it, and the capability does not.

This is the student-facing twin of the deskilling card below, and it is the failure mode most likely to be invisible in your marks for a term or two before it is visible in your vivas.

▲Borrowed valuesnewThe output has a politics you did not choosewhat to do with this

Model outputs carry positions — on contested questions in your discipline, on what counts as a good argument, on which authorities are worth citing. Reports of value drift in agent populations are a vivid version of an ordinary fact: you did not choose those positions and you have not audited them.

The practical form: when you generate a reading list, a seminar question or a case, ask what position it has quietly taken, and whether you would have taken it.

■DeskillingThis one compoundsthe reason the other five matter

The judgement you stop exercising is the judgement you need in order to evaluate the output. The competence that lets you audit the tool is the same competence the tool relieves you of using, and nothing warns you when it goes.

The practical response is not abstinence. It is deciding, in advance and in writing, which parts of your work you will keep doing by hand precisely because you need to stay able to do them.

New in v4 — and it changes the response. Ferdman (2026) argues that deskilling is structural rather than individual, and gives it a name: a capacity-hostile environment is one in which the mediation of a system blocks the slow cultivation of a capacity. The consequence for you is uncomfortable and useful. A private resolution to keep marking by hand does not survive a workload model, a submission deadline and a tool that is already in the VLE. What survives is a change to the environment: a task that is not routed through the tool, a review step that a person has to sign, a module design in which the difficulty is the point. Write the environment, not the intention.

▸AdministrationStart institutional: a platform with an agreement, or a local modeltools and their faults

Institutional and national platforms. Several countries now run their own front ends for higher education: SURF's EduGenAI in the Netherlands, a national pilot with open-source and commercial models, due for general availability around the end of 2026 and explicitly not for assessment or fraud detection; GWDG's Chat AI in Germany, reached through the academic cloud; and self-hosted open-source front ends such as Open WebUI or HAWKI, which an institution runs in front of whichever model it licenses. The virtue of all of them is the agreement and the retention rules, not the model. The fault: the model catalogue changes, so a prompt tuned last term may behave differently this term and you will not be told — and somebody has to run the service.

Licensed education editions of the large assistants. Microsoft 365 Copilot Chat with enterprise data protection, Gemini for Education and NotebookLM in Google Workspace for Education, ChatGPT Edu and Claude for Education. Where your institution has signed, these come with a contract that excludes training on your data and gives administrators control over retention. Their faults: the providers sit outside the EU, so EU data residency settles where the data is stored and not every question about who can reach it; features and terms move faster than your institution's review; and the more of your teaching you build on one, the higher the exit cost.

European and open models. Mistral (France; the Le Chat assistant and models served from European infrastructure), Apertus (Switzerland; ETH Zurich, EPFL and CSCS; fully open, Apache 2.0, trained on more than a thousand languages) and EuroLLM (EU-funded, open, all 24 official EU languages). You reach them through an institutional platform, an API or a laptop. Their virtue is that you can see what they are. Their fault is that the open ones trail the frontier on hard tasks, and somebody still has to host them.

Local models via Ollama or LM Studio — the route this session's lab uses. Its virtue is that nothing leaves the machine. Its faults are real: smaller models are meaningfully weaker at long documents and at languages other than English, and a laptop without a capable GPU will be slow enough to change how you work.

▸PreparationWhere the gain is largest and the risk smallesttools and their faults

Drafting task variants, alternative worked examples, reading-list annotations, question banks, plain-language versions of a dense handout. You are the reviewer before anything reaches a student, which is what makes this family safe.

Transcription — open-weight speech models run locally handle recorded lectures without sending audio anywhere. They do badly on discipline-specific vocabulary and on strong regional accents, and they will hallucinate through silence rather than leave a gap.

Translation — an EU-based service such as DeepL is a defensible route for teaching material. It is not a defensible route for a student's submitted work; see the copyright panel.

The trap in this family is volume. Producing four versions of a task is easy; deciding which one to use is the work, and it did not get easier.

▲In-classStudents are present and cannot easily opt outtools and one hard stop

Live captioning and translation are the clearest accessibility gains available in this list, and they degrade in exactly the situation where they matter most: a noisy room, an unfamiliar accent, technical vocabulary. Announce them as an aid, never as a record.

The hard stop: anything that infers emotion from faces, voices or posture in an educational setting. This is not a matter of taste or local policy — see the legal panel.

Attendance, engagement scoring and participation analytics belong to this family too, and they are where an in-class tool quietly becomes an evaluation tool.

■EvaluationThe output lands on somebody's recordtools, and what not to do

Rubric and criteria drafting is the safe end: you are writing the instrument, not applying it.

Feedback drafting sits in the middle and depends entirely on the route the student's text takes — see the copyright panel below.

Marking support is the end where the law arrives. A grade is a decision about a person, and the two questions that follow are whether a human is genuinely in the loop and whether the student can contest it.

Detection is treated separately below, because the argument against it is not about accuracy.

The legal panel

Five provisions, and one date that moved

  • Art. 4 — AI literacy. The duty falls on the deploying organisation, which for teaching purposes is your institution. It is discharged by staff who can explain the tools they use, not by a procurement decision. In application since February 2025, and not deferred.
  • Art. 5(1)(f) — prohibited practice. Inferring emotions from a person in the workplace or in education, outside narrow medical and safety exceptions. A prohibition, in application since February 2025, and not deferred. If a supplier demonstrates engagement detection to your faculty, this is the article to name in the room.
  • Art. 50 — transparency. People must be told when they are interacting with an AI system or when content is artificially generated. In application since 2 August 2026. If a chatbot answers your students' module questions, this is your duty and not the vendor's alone.
  • Art. 14 — human oversight. Attaches to high-risk systems: oversight has to be real, informed, and capable of overriding the output. "A human clicked approve" is not the test.
  • Annex III §3 — education. Admission and assignment to institutions, evaluating learning outcomes, assessing the appropriate level of education, and monitoring prohibited behaviour during tests. These are the four listed situations, and grading is squarely among them.

The date that moved. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026. It defers the obligations attaching to standalone Annex III high-risk systems from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and Annex I embedded systems to 2 August 2028. The stated reason is the late arrival of harmonised standards, not a change of view about the risk. Articles 4, 5 and 50 were not deferred.

Read the deferral as sixteen extra months to build the process, not as sixteen months in which the question does not apply. The obligations are unchanged; only the date they bite has moved.

GDPR Art. 22, in one sentence for a colleague

A person has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing which produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them — and a mark is the standard example anyone will reach for. Meaningful human involvement is the condition, and it has to be involvement by someone with the authority and the information to reach a different conclusion.

Detectors

The case against them is not that they are inaccurate. It is that they are unfalsifiable in use and that their errors fall unevenly.

■Asymmetric harmThe two errors are not comparablethe argument

A missed case costs a mark that should have been lower. A false accusation costs a student a disciplinary process, a relationship with their department, and in some cases a visa. Any instrument whose two error types carry consequences this unequal has to be justified against that asymmetry, not against an average.

■UnfalsifiabilityThe accused cannot produce disproofthe argument

Ask what evidence a student could bring that would settle the matter in their favour. Version history and drafts help and are worth requiring, but they are also producible by anyone who anticipated the accusation. A procedure in which the accused cannot construct a disproof is not an academic-integrity procedure, whatever it is called. See Bassett et al. (2026) in the reading list.

■Uneven errorsDistributed by first languagethe argument

Detector scores respond to features that correlate with writing that is fluent but unadventurous: shorter clauses, conventional collocations, low lexical surprise. Those are also features of competent academic writing by someone working in their second, third or fourth language. The consequence is not that the tool is imprecise. It is that its errors fall on the students least able to contest an accusation. An instrument whose false positives are systematically distributed by first language is not a weak instrument; it is a different instrument from the one it claims to be.

If someone asks for the accuracy figures: decline, and say why. Quoting a percentage concedes that the question is empirical and invites a rebuttal with a better percentage next term. The objection survives any accuracy rate.

Where the payment note becomes an assessment problem

The strongest general models are, at present, the paid ones. If a task can be done better with a subscription than without, the assessment is measuring disposable income alongside whatever it was meant to measure — and the effect is invisible in the marks.

  • You cannot require a tool your institution does not provide. Not as a matter of policy preference, but as a matter of what you are entitled to make a condition of assessment.
  • Where the institution does provide a compliant option, providing it quickly is the equity intervention. Every term without one is a term of quiet stratification.
  • If your task design assumes AI use, the fairness of that assumption rests on two conditions: everyone has been taught the use, and everyone has comparable access. Neither is satisfied by announcing that AI is permitted.

The direction nobody checks — copyright in students' work

Copyright in a student's examination script, term paper or thesis belongs to the student. Pasting it into a general commercial assistant to draft feedback, check similarity or summarise it is a use of someone else's protected work, made by you, usually without their knowledge, and frequently under terms permitting the provider to retain the text. This is separate from the data-protection question and is not resolved by anonymising the file, because the work itself is the protected thing.

Practical position: student work goes to the institutional or local option, or it does not go. If you intend to use any external service on submitted work, say so in the module handbook before submission, not in the feedback afterwards.

Feedback: the choice between two failuresnew

Krommer (2026) frames automated feedback as a passage between two hazards, and the framing survives translation. Feedback general enough to be safely generated is too general to change what a student does. Feedback specific enough to change what a student does is specific enough that you have to be able to defend every sentence of it — which means reading the work, which was the part you were trying to avoid.

The useful consequence is not that automated feedback is impossible. It is that the gain is in drafting against a skill prompt — a standing instruction — that you wrote and can defend, and never in the last step before it reaches the student.

Agents change what an online test measuresnew

The 2026 argument, put bluntly: a tool that browses, reads a course page, opens a PDF and submits a form does not just answer a question faster — it dissolves the assumption that an unproctored online test measures the person sitting at the keyboard. Field notes from practitioners describe the same thing arriving in ordinary classrooms rather than in a lab.

Two responses that do not work: detection (see below) and a longer honour code. The response that does work is a format in which the process is the evidence — a design question that needs more than a paragraph, and more than this session.

The prompting evolution — five stagesnew in v5

The stages are not a quality ladder: a single stage-one answer can be as good as anything above it. They differ in what survives the session, in how much of the work the system does without you — and in what you now have to govern.
①Stage 1 — PromptWhere everyone startswhen it is the right stage

Perfectly appropriate for a one-off: rephrase this paragraph, list six discussion questions, translate this notice. Correcting your way through a chat — six exchanges until it is right — is still stage one: something usable, nothing reusable, and the knowledge of what made it good lives in a conversation you will not open again.

The error is not using stage one. The error is using it for something you will do forty more times.

②Stage 2 — Skill promptWrite the instruction down oncewhat to put in it

Name the audience, the scope, the count and the prohibition. The example used in the lab: address the writing, never the writer; quote the words at issue; two points maximum; end with one answerable question; do not rewrite the passage.

"Skill" is now also the agent tools' own word: a saved instruction file, loaded when a task matches it — the SKILL.md format Anthropic published as an open standard in December 2025 and other vendors adopted. Whatever the format, it is a colleague-readable artefact that a programme team can review, dispute, correct and reuse next year. For most teaching staff this is the stage that pays off this term.

③Stage 3 — Harness prompt (for agents)The specification an agent works insidewhat it buys

An agent is a model inside a program — the harness — that lets it read files, call tools and act in several steps. The harness prompt is the specification that governs it: which inputs, which tools, what it must write down, when it must stop and ask. The "agent mode" in the commercial assistants is a harness someone else wrote; ask to see its specification and you usually cannot.

In the lab it is deliberately plain: one written specification, a folder of inputs, one output per input, each with a header recording the input, the specification, the model, the settings and the time. The difference from stage one is not cleverness. It is a trail somebody else can audit.

④Stage 4 — Loop promptAct, check, revise — until a stop conditionwhat it costs

The agent runs its own correction loop: draft, check the draft against written rules, revise what failed, check again — until the checks pass or a limit is reached. What makes it safe is not the model. It is the checks and the stop condition, and both are yours to write.

The costs are specific. Every round is another inference, which is where the resource panel stops being abstract. A loop optimises for passing its checks, not for the student — crude checks produce text that satisfies crude checks. And a model checking its own draft brings its own blind spots to the checking, which is sycophancy turned inward.

⑤Stage 5 — Wiki for a self-learning setupThe system writes down what it learnedwhat to govern

The model's weights do not change — that is the training-versus-inference row above. What changes is a set of pages the system writes and reads back next time: lessons, corrections, conventions, summaries of sources. Karpathy's LLM wiki (2026) made the pattern popular — raw sources stay untouched, the agent maintains a wiki of linked pages, a schema file says how. The built-in "memory" of the commercial assistants is the same idea with the pages hidden.

Governance is the whole question. Whoever can edit the wiki steers every later answer; an error written into it compounds; student data must never go into it. A wiki you can read, version and correct is a governance object. A memory you cannot read is not.

3.4 · A worked example from the upper stages

The upper stages stay abstract until you see something built with a written specification. Here is one, built at this university, in the open.

Circular construction safety and material twin

A browser-based simulation for vocational construction teaching. Live regional weather, a fault developing across eight sensor channels, three role consultations in sequence, and six material passports the learner routes to reuse, repair, recycle or quarantine — with retained mass, avoided carbon and avoided replacement cost moving against a circularity score. The learner's output is a diagnosis, an action and a written justification. It runs from a static web host with no backend and no key.

What made that possible — and what did not

Not learning to code in the sense of a degree, and not prompting in the sense of clever phrases. Two literacies, doing different jobs.

Three decisions in it that are pedagogical, not technical

1 · The architecture is the privacy answer. No login, no accounts, no server-side storage: the learner's decisions never leave the browser. Privacy by design, made structural rather than promised — there is no personal data to protect because the design does not collect any. One caveat stated honestly: it does fetch public regional weather, which is one external call, and no personal data goes out with it.

2 · The data labelling is a teaching artefact. Public weather values are marked as live external data; the concrete, strain, crane-tilt, dust and electrical channels are marked virtual until physical sensors are connected; impact figures are marked educational estimates rather than engineering forecasts. Learners see the provenance of every number they are asked to act on. Most simulations do not tell you which of their numbers are real, and learners generalise from that silence.

3 · The output is a decision record. A diagnosis, an action and a written justification: a process artefact, timestamped and attributable. That is exactly what an assessment redesign needs.

The seduction to resist

An artefact like this is enjoyable to build, and that is its principal risk. Before the second one, answer three questions in writing: what teaching problem does this solve that a well-run seminar does not; what does it replace rather than add; and who maintains it when the person who specified it is on sabbatical. A repository nobody has opened since the demo is not an OER. It is a hobby with a licence file.

Provenance, licensing, and where the artefact lives

Where a substantial part of an artefact was generated, your credibility rests on the audit trail being public: who contributed what, in which commit, on which date. A single file emailed round has none of that; a repository has all of it by default. Which is why where you put the artefact is part of the pedagogical decision and not an afterthought. If the work is a fork, the fork relationship belongs on the repository page rather than in a footnote.

The transferable move

Nothing above is specific to construction. The pattern: pick one thing your students cannot currently practise because the real setting is too dangerous, too slow, too expensive or too rare; specify it as a system with an explicit constraint set; build it in small versioned passes you can actually read; label what is real and what is simulated; make the learner's output a justification rather than a score. Welding, clinical triage, laboratory safety, negotiation, network incident response, ward handover — the list in your discipline is longer than you think.

Where an artefact like that sits legally

No personal data, so the data-protection questions do not arise in the first place. Nothing evaluates a learner for an outcome that affects them, so it is not the Annex III §3 situation — it becomes one the moment you attach the score to a grade. Art. 50 applies to what the learner is told: if a consultation response is generated rather than authored, say so on screen. And Art. 4 is the reason you are the right person to build it, since the literacy duty falls on the deploying organisation and is discharged by staff who can explain their own tools.

3.5 · Three positions worth holdingnew

◆AI as a reflection instrumentThe stage-two move for student writingthe pattern

Rather than asking a system to improve a draft, ask it to interrogate one: name the claim this paragraph makes, list what would have to be true for it to hold, say which of those the text does not establish. The student keeps authorship and gets the questions a supervisor would have asked in week four rather than week eleven.

This is a skill prompt, not a one-off request — which puts it at stage two and makes it something a programme team can adopt, disagree with and correct.

◆The misunderstanding that blocks the workOn academic writing and generative systemsthe argument

Wampfler (2026) argues that teaching development is stuck on a false premise: that academic writing is the transcription of finished thought, so that automating the transcription is either harmless or fatal depending on your temperament. If writing is instead the process by which the thought is formed, both positions collapse, and the question becomes which parts of that formation the student must go through personally.

That question you can actually answer for your own module, and the answer is the assessment design.

▲A book that is its own case studyRead it on the meta levelwhat to notice

There is now a German-language book on higher education in the age of generative AI whose companion material — slides, podcast episodes — is entirely AI-generated. The observation circulating with it in our own Fokusgruppe is that the companion material demonstrates precisely the problems such material has, and that concrete recommendations for action remain scarce.

Take that as the honest test for anything produced this term, including this page: does it tell you what to do on Monday, and can you tell which parts were generated? If the answer to the second is no, the first is worth less.

Verification tooling, and the loop it createsnew

Fabricated references are now common enough to be reported as a measurable trend, and a small industry of checkers has appeared to catch them — reference-verification services, and research tools for detecting citation hallucination at scale.

Use them. Then notice the shape of what you have just done: you have answered a tool’s failure with another tool, which has its own false-positive rate, its own data route and its own obligation to be explained under Art. 4. That is not an argument against using it. It is the reason the checker belongs in your decision record alongside the thing it checks.

Closing check — six items

Which statement about the December 2027 date is correct?

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 moved the Annex III standalone high-risk date from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 because harmonised standards were late. The prohibitions, the literacy duty and the transparency regime were untouched, and education remains in Annex III §3.

What does running a model locally remove?

Hosting answers the transfer question and nothing else. If the output feeds a grade it is the same Annex III situation wherever it runs, the transparency duty attaches to what the learner is told, and the literacy duty falls on the deploying organisation regardless of route.

A colleague says detectors are now accurate enough to use. What is the strongest reply?

Citing a rate concedes that the question is empirical, and invites a better rate next term. The unfalsifiability objection and the uneven distribution of false positives survive any accuracy figure.

Which of these is a stage-two artefact — a skill prompt?

Stage two is the skill prompt: written once, readable by a colleague, reusable next year. The six-turn conversation is still stage one. The last option is stage three, which adds the folder, the provenance headers and the version history.

Which failure mode is not caught by checking the output?

The first, second and fourth are all detectable by someone competent in the domain. Deskilling removes that competence gradually, and nothing signals when it has gone — which is why the response has to be written down in advance rather than noticed in time.

Why are only two areas allowed on the 90-day plan?

The limit is practical rather than prescribed. A five-area plan is abandoned in week three, and participants read the abandonment as a personal failure rather than as a scoping error — which makes them less likely to use the framework again.

Six lines to take away

  • A token is not a word, and the context window is a boundary rather than a setting.
  • Fluency is not evidence of grounding, and no amount of rereading the output will settle it.
  • Hosting answers the transfer question and none of the others.
  • Prohibitions are not conditions: Art. 5(1)(f) is a stop, not a risk assessment.
  • The stage you work at decides what still exists next term — and what you now have to govern.
  • Tools, strategies, reflection — in that order, or the first two do not hold.

That is a feature when you send them to a colleague who teaches a workshop-based subject, and a limitation when you send them to a lawyer. Point people at the module, then run this session’s Block 2 and Block 3 with them, because those are the two the modules do not reach.

Quick check — the placement map

You run every model locally. Which claim does the framework support?

Route answers the transfer question and nothing else. Annex III §3, Art. 50 and Art. 4 are indifferent to where the bytes went, which is why the router puts the legal competence in the amber column for that practice rather than the green one.

A colleague uses no AI at all and says the framework does not apply to them. What does the map say?

Deciding not to use AI for a task, for a stated reason, is the Manage-domain competence in its own right. Deciding by default is not. Meanwhile their students are using it, their reference manager contains it, and the Art. 4 literacy duty falls on the institution and is discharged by staff who can explain the tools in use.
